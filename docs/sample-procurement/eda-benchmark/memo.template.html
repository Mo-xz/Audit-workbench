What the procurement data shows

A reading of the six procure-to-pay tables before any test is designed: what the population is, where the money goes, how the cycle runs, who holds authority, and where the records break their own pattern.

§Summary

  1. A small, complete, one-year cycle that reconciles on one line. Every requisition maps to at most one PO and every PO to exactly one invoice. PKR 645.4M was requisitioned, 35.4M of it was never ordered, invoices ran 12.6M above their POs, and 622.5M was paid. Nothing is open: every PO is closed, every GRN received, every invoice paid.
  2. Value sits in a few large purchases, one requester and five vendors. The 18 requisitions over 10M carry 86% of the value. The Facilities Manager alone raised 45% of it. Five of twelve vendors received 79% of payments.
  3. Authority is used by band, almost mechanically, which puts the CEO on most of the money. Approvals follow the matrix bands so closely that the CEO signed 77% of requisition value and 80% of payments. The five breaches are lower-limit approvers signing one band up; four of them are the Head of Treasury.
  4. The records behave like keyed data more than system events. Every PO equals its requisition estimate to the rupee. Thirteen of fourteen items carry a single unit price across every order and vendor. And 27% of all dates fall on a weekend, which is what dates ignoring the calendar would give. Tests built on estimates or day counts measure what was typed.
  5. Departments and vendors do not describe what was bought. Human Resources bought three data-centre UPS replacements (108.8M paid). A marketing agency supplied a branch fit-out and onboarding kits. The largest vendor, an IT hardware firm, was paid for seven different things, including security consulting.
  6. The cycle is fast and pays early. A requisition is paid a median 47 days after it is raised. Every invoice was paid before its due date, a median 13 days early on 30-day terms.
  7. The exceptions cluster at the payment step. Of fourteen exception patterns, four sit where an invoice is released: paid with no goods receipt reference, paid before the goods arrived, paid with no approval, and billed above the PO. One chain, 2024127, combines a unit price 37.5 times the norm with two authority breaches.

1The data received

Six tables arrived: three transaction tables that form one chain, and three masters that the chain refers to. The chain is unusually clean. Requisition, PO and invoice numbers move in lockstep (REQ2024n becomes PO2024n and INV2024n), each PO cites exactly one requisition, and each invoice cites exactly one PO. There are no split orders, no part-invoicing and no credit notes.

What kind of data this is

Four properties of the records matter more for test design than any single exception.

  • The estimate is not an estimate. All 52 POs equal their requisition's estimated cost exactly. Either the PO value overwrites the estimate, or the PO is raised from it without negotiation. A test that the PO stays within the estimate will pass on every row and prove nothing.
  • Prices are a catalogue. Thirteen of fourteen items have one unit price on every PO, whichever of two to five vendors supplied them (Exhibit 12). Price variance across vendors cannot be analysed here; only a price that breaks the catalogue stands out.
  • Dates ignore the working week. Of 566 dated events across the chain, 155 (27.4%) fall on a Saturday or Sunday, against 28.6% if dates were spread at random across the week (Exhibit 2). Approval and payment dates look keyed rather than stamped by a system. Day-count tests remain useful for finding order breaks, but they measure the dates as entered.
  • One chain sits outside every series. REQ2024009 became PO2024004 and INV2024004, while every other chain shares one number. Its vendor invoice number, VINV001-202404, is the only one not in the VINVnnn-2024 form. It is also the only chain where the invoice predates the PO, and the only requisition approved one band above its value (by the CEO, for 2.0M).

2Where the money goes

The requisition values run from 36,000 to 85M, with a median of 3.0M. The distribution is steep: a third of the requisitions carry almost nine tenths of the value, and the 17 requisitions under 1M add up to 1.1% of it. Any sample selected by value is, in effect, a review of 18 purchases.

By department

Facilities is the largest spender by a wide margin: nine requisitions, 291.8M, 45% of the total, all raised by one person, the Facilities Manager. That includes three of the four largest purchases in the year (85M, 74M and 74M). Procurement raises the most requisitions (19) but at a median of 1.9M; its two Requisition and PO Associates appear to raise orders on behalf of other functions.

By vendor

Twelve vendors were paid. The top five took 79% of payments, and Vertex IT Hardware alone took 28% across seven invoices. Two vendors that the master marks as not active were ordered from and paid: RapidBuild Constructors (Under Review, 5.25M) and Express Courier Services (Inactive, 0.95M). The master records no date for either status, so it cannot say whether the status changed before or after the order.

What each department bought

Read against the items, neither the department nor the vendor describes the purchase. Human Resources bought three of the four data-centre UPS replacements, including the two largest (64M and 32M). Marketing bought a core banking licence renewal and a UPS replacement. Facilities bought two security consulting engagements worth 95.2M. On the supply side, every item came from two to five vendors, and vendors supplied up to seven unrelated items each. Before any test leans on department or vendor as a category, the client should say what the department field records: the function that needed the goods, the budget holder, or the person who keyed the requisition.

Over the year

Commitments come in two waves: January to March, and September to November. May to August is quiet, and no requisition at all was raised in May. Payments follow commitments by about a month, so cash goes out in two peaks, March (205.7M) and October (166.2M). Nothing in the profile looks like a year-end rush. December commitments are modest (32.9M) and the payments made in January and February 2025 all relate to POs raised in November and December.

3How the cycle runs

Each requisition approval step takes one to four days. Raising the PO after financial approval takes up to 13 days (median 6.5), delivery takes 5 to 25 days, and payment follows invoice approval by 3 to 20 days. End to end, a requisition is paid a median 47 days after it is raised, and never sooner than 19 or later than 64.

The value of a timing profile is that it says what normal is, and so how abnormal a break is. Two steps run backwards for some records. Four invoices are dated before the goods receipt, three of them by exactly 23 days. Two invoices were recorded as received before their own invoice date. And the normal range makes the three requisitions that were never ordered stand out: approved 87 to 154 days before year end, when every other requisition became a PO within 13 days.

Every one of the 52 invoices was paid before its due date: between 4 and 21 days early, a median of 13 days, on 30-day terms. The bank is financing its suppliers for roughly two weeks on every invoice. That is a treasury question rather than a control failure. It also means there are no late payments to explain, and no pressure on the payment step that would excuse skipping a match.

4Who does what

Twenty staff appear in the staff file and nineteen of them act in the chain. Each role is held by a small, fixed group (Exhibit 11). Nine people raise requisitions. Four Finance staff verify them. Two sourcing managers give every procurement approval. Four executives give every financial approval and every invoice approval, each within a value band. The Head of Procurement acts in no role at all during the year.

Two patterns run through the whole population rather than a few rows. First, in all 48 chains that Finance verified, the same person verified the requisition at the start and the invoice at the end. That is one person owning a chain end to end, which reduces the independence of the invoice check. Second, the four requisitions that Finance did not verify were verified by the person who raised them. That is the requester stepping into the Finance role, which is why those four are exceptions and not noise.

Routing by band concentrates authority. The CEO gave 18 financial approvals worth 496.6M (77% of requisition value) and 16 invoice approvals worth 495.0M (80% of payments). Whatever evidence supports the CEO's approval is the most valuable single document in the file. The breaches are not random either: four of the five are the Head of Treasury, whose limit is 1M, signing requisitions and invoices of 2.3M to 4.2M. The fifth is the CFO approving a 64M requisition against a 10M limit, while the invoice for the same purchase went correctly to the CEO.

5Prices

Because prices are a fixed catalogue, a price that departs from it is unmistakable. PO2024127 bought one New Hire Onboarding Kit for 3,000,000. The three other orders for the same kit paid 80,000 a unit, so this one is 37.5 times the price. The order went to Apex Marketing Group, and the same chain carries two of the authority breaches in Exhibit 10.

6Exceptions found

These are the records that break the patterns above. Values are the full amount of each record unless stated, and a record can appear under more than one heading. They are listed for testing, not concluded: each needs the documents behind it before it is a finding.

One chain carries three exceptions. The Head of Treasury approved REQ2024127 at 3.0M against a 1M limit. PO2024127 bought one onboarding kit at 37.5 times the catalogue price. The Head of Treasury then approved INV2024127, again above the limit. Each check, run alone, flags one row. Read together, they describe one purchase that passed three controls it should have failed.

7What the data cannot answer

Some questions an auditor would ask of a procure-to-pay cycle have no field anywhere in these tables. They are gaps in the evidence, not clean results.

  • Was what arrived what was ordered? There is an ordered quantity but no received quantity, so short or partial delivery cannot be seen.
  • Was what was paid what was invoiced? There is no payment amount or payment reference, so over-payment and duplicate payment cannot be tested.
  • Who raised the PO, and who keyed the invoice? Neither table names its preparer.
  • When did a vendor's status change? The master holds a current status only, with no bank, tax or onboarding details.
  • Which limit applied on the day? The approval matrix has no effective dates.
  • Was a requisition withdrawn? The status field has only Approved and Completed.

8Implications for the audit plan

Where to point the testing

  • Test the 18 requisitions over 10M individually; they are 86% of the value. Cover the other 37 with whole-population rules, which this data supports well.
  • Test the payment release step on every invoice: goods receipt link, approval, amount against PO, and payment date against receipt. That is where the exceptions cluster.
  • Obtain the evidence behind the CEO's approvals; 16 invoices worth 495M rest on them.
  • Corroborate the dates behind every timing exception to source documents before relying on them, since dates appear keyed.
  • Drop "PO within estimate" as a test. It cannot fail on this data.

Questions for management

  1. Does the system enforce approval limits, or does the user choose the approver?
  2. Is the requisition estimate overwritten when the PO is raised?
  3. Are dates stamped by the system or keyed by staff?
  4. What does the requesting department field record?
  5. Are vendors approved for particular categories of goods and services?
  6. Why does chain 2024009/2024004 sit outside the numbering series?
  7. Is paying two weeks early a policy, or the result of a discount?
